# Choose the Smart Review Analyzer, deliberately differentiated

Rachit and Manan should build **Project 1, the Smart Review Analyzer**. It is the only one of the three candidates where **all eight lab experiments appear as natural features** rather than bolt-ons. Its main dataset (IMDB 50K) is clean, balanced and loads in one line, and every core component runs on a CPU laptop with no GPU, paid API or PDF parsing. The obvious weakness is that review sentiment is the most common student project. The fix is not a different topic but a sharper build: one evaluation table comparing lexicon, classical-ML and transformer models on the same split (no published source does this), dependency-rule aspect sentiment, and pros/cons summarization on Indian (Flipkart) product data. **Project 12, the Plagiarism / Text Similarity Checker, is a strong runner-up.** It is technically the lightest, has the cleanest evaluation story and makes N-grams central, but it covers sentiment and summarization only weakly, and its key dataset's original host is down. **Project 3, the Resume–JD Matcher, should be ruled out.** It covers two experiments weakly, its only labelled match benchmark has unknown provenance and defeats even fine-tuned DistilBERT (about 50% accuracy), and it carries PDF-extraction, privacy and bias risks that eat into a 16-day window (20 Oct to 5 Nov 2026).

## Only the review analyzer exercises all eight lab experiments natively

A lab mini-project is graded largely on whether it shows the techniques taught in lab, so syllabus coverage is the deciding criterion. The research notes map each project against the eight experiments (CSV + graphs, tokenization, preprocessing/morphology, word count/word cloud/NER, POS/chunking, N-gram LM + smoothing, sentiment/subjectivity, summarization). The pattern is clear:

| Lab experiment | Review Analyzer (1) | Resume–JD Matcher (3) | Plagiarism Checker (12) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. CSV + graphs | Strong: class distribution, confusion matrices, aspect charts | Strong: category bars, score box plots | Strong: ROC/PR curves, score boxplots, N×N heatmap |
| 2. Tokenization | Strong | Strong | Strong: sentence alignment, word shingles |
| 3. Preprocessing / morphology | Strong, with a negation-stopword ablation | Strong: lemma-normalised skill matching | Strong: preprocessing ablation on Jaccard/TF-IDF |
| 4. Word count / word cloud / NER | Strong: brands as ORG/PRODUCT | Strong: ORG, DATE, custom SKILL entity | Moderate: shared-vs-unique word clouds, NER overlap |
| 5. POS / chunking | **Strong: the aspect extractor is built on POS and dependencies** | Strong: noun-chunk skill candidates | Partial: content-word filtering |
| 6. N-gram LM + smoothing | Moderate: bigram features, perplexity classifier | **Weak, needs a bolt-on** | **Strong: N-grams are the core method** |
| 7. Sentiment / subjectivity | **Strong: the core feature** | **Weak, needs a bolt-on** | **Weak** |
| 8. Summarization | **Strong: pros/cons summaries, ROUGE** | Moderate: JD summary | **Weak / optional** |

The review analyzer is a direct continuation of experiments 4, 5, 7 and 8. The notes' feature-to-tool map assigns pandas/matplotlib, NLTK, spaCy, `wordcloud`, VADER/TextBlob, scikit-learn and `sumy` to the eight experiments one-to-one. Its single soft spot, experiment 6, has a cheap, syllabus-faithful fix: train a Laplace-smoothed bigram LM on positive reviews and another on negative reviews, then classify a new review by which LM gives it lower perplexity. The other two projects each need two features invented just to tick boxes. For the resume matcher these are "subjectivity of resume summary lines" and a perplexity-based category classifier. For the plagiarism checker they are "sentiment preservation across paraphrases" and "summary of the matched region." Examiners usually spot artificial hooks like these.

The best case for the plagiarism checker is worth stating fairly. N-grams are its foundation (shingling, containment, winnowing k-grams), so experiment 6 is covered more deeply than in any other option. It also has the most rigorous evaluation design. If the instructor has signalled that sentiment projects are overdone, that argument wins and Project 12 becomes the pick. Without such a signal, coverage of experiments 7 and 8 matters more than depth on experiment 6.

## Datasets and evaluation favour the projects with real labels

### Review analyzer: a standard benchmark plus a star-rated product set

**IMDB** has **25,000 train, 25,000 test and 50,000 unlabeled reviews with binary labels**, citing Maas et al. 2011 ([HF dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/stanfordnlp/imdb)). The Kaggle 50K CSV is balanced but contains HTML `<br />` noise that needs cleaning ([Medium walkthrough](https://khalidsiddique2401.medium.com/imdb-sentiment-analysis-using-python-63a5d494f42c)). IMDB has **no neutral class**, so a 3-class experiment needs star-rated product data. Amazon Fine Food Reviews has **568,454 reviews with 1–5 star scores and a human-written Summary field** ([manish-vi notebooks](https://github.com/manish-vi/supervised_ml_notebooks)). That field has already been used as a summarization target ([jalammar](https://github.com/jalammar/Text-Summarization-with-Amazon-Reviews)), which gives the students ROUGE references for free. For Indian relevance, the Flipkart "Product Reviews With Sentiment" set has about 205k rows, but **its sentiment labels were produced by running VADER on the Summary column** ([Baselight snippet](https://baselight.app/u/kaggle/dataset/niraliivaghani_flipkart_product_customer_reviews_dataset)). Evaluating VADER against those labels would be circular, so the star `Rate` column must serve as ground truth. Aspect extraction can be checked against **SemEval-2014 Task 4**, which has over 6K laptop and restaurant sentences with human aspect annotations ([SemEval](https://alt.qcri.org/semeval2014/task4/)). A hand-labelled set of 50–100 sentences also works.

The evaluation plan follows from those datasets. The team should report accuracy, precision, recall and F1 for VADER, TextBlob, TF-IDF + MNB/LR/LinearSVC (unigram vs uni+bigram) and an optional DistilBERT run on a 1–2k test subset, with confusion matrices for each. Add a preprocessing ablation, a 3-class product experiment, ROUGE-1/2/L for summaries against a lead-sentence baseline using `rouge-score` ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/rouge-score/)), and a manual precision/recall check of aspect extraction. The published numbers give a clear ladder to compare against. TF-IDF + logistic regression reaches about **88–89% on IMDB** ([CAUCHY 2025](https://ejournal.uin-malang.ac.id/index.php/Math/article/view/33809); [SIMAD](https://repository.simad.edu.so/id/eprint/31)). Bigram NB-SVM reaches **91.22%** ([Wang & Manning 2012](https://aclanthology.org/P12-2018/)). Off-the-shelf DistilBERT-SST2 drops to **80.6% on IMDB versus 96.0%** for a RoBERTa-large model trained on diverse review data ([siebert card](https://huggingface.co/siebert/sentiment-roberta-large-english)). Lexicon tools score lower on movie reviews; one cited study puts VADER at 77% and TextBlob at 74% ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3935/Paper15.pdf)). Those figures come from different papers with different preprocessing, so the students' own same-split table fills a real gap.

### Plagiarism checker: purpose-built labels, but a dead download link

The **Clough & Stevenson corpus** is purpose-built for this task. It has short answers at four obfuscation levels (near copy, light revision, heavy revision, non-plagiarised) for five CS topics ([arXiv 2605.15110](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.15110)). However, **its original Sheffield host did not resolve in October 2026**. A 100-text CSV mirror survives inside the Pattern library's test corpora ([git.xpub.nl mirror](https://git.xpub.nl/Bohye.Woo/bo-graduation/src/commit/52ca77ce4db530594376462d9c69a6f81193ae26/nltk-book/pattern-master/test/corpora)). **MRPC** (about 5.8k sentence pairs) loads in one line from GLUE ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/nyu-mll/glue)), but its test set is about **67% paraphrase**, so accuracy has to be judged against a majority baseline ([CEUR parmex](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3202/parmex-paper2.pdf)). **PAWS** (108,463 high-overlap pairs, on which earlier models scored under 40%) makes a good stress test for showing why lexical methods fail ([arXiv 1904.01130](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01130?context=cs)). Evaluation works naturally: treat each similarity score as a thresholded classifier, tune the threshold on a train split, and plot ROC/PR curves for all methods. Thresholds must be tuned per method, because optimal embedding-cosine cut-offs on MRPC range from **0.334 to 0.867** across models ([Computers 2025 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/07b42863f3ea496cb3572f121c7543cc)). The honest headline will be that unsupervised methods barely beat the majority baseline on MRPC. That is defensible, but it is a less satisfying result than a clear accuracy ladder.

### Resume matcher: plenty of text, almost no trustworthy ground truth

Resume data is easy to find: 962 resumes in 25 categories ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2021/06/resume-screening-with-natural-language-processing-in-python/)), about 2,484 in 24 categories ([Baselight snippet](https://baselight.app/u/kaggle/dataset/snehaanbhawal_resume_dataset)), and ESCO's **13,939 skills** for a skill dictionary ([ESCO v1.2](https://esco.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2024-05/V1.2%20Launch%20-%20ESCO%20SEC%20combined%20-%20final%20clean.pdf)). The trouble is that the project's headline output, the match score, has almost nothing to validate it against. The only labelled resume–JD pair set (Good/Potential/No fit) is one where **fine-tuned DistilBERT reaches about 50% accuracy and 0.43 macro-F1, and a TF-IDF baseline matched or beat it** ([domynom model card](https://huggingface.co/domynom/resume-job-distilbert)). Whether its labels were written by humans or generated synthetically is unknown. Category classifiers on the 962-resume set report about **99% accuracy** ([SWU thesis](https://ir-ithesis.swu.ac.th/handle/123456789/2762)). The notes suspect duplicate rows inflate that figure but did not verify it, so the students would need to test it. Credible evaluation therefore needs the two students to hand-annotate 50 resume–JD pairs and report Cohen's kappa, Spearman correlation and nDCG. That is rigorous, but it is slow, and it rests on two undergraduates' judgement of hiring fit.

## Every idea has been built before, so the evaluation is the differentiator

All three spaces have existing open-source projects, and examiners know it. For reviews, `amri-tah/Amazon-Reviews-Sentiment-Analysis` offers single-review, CSV-batch and word-cloud modes but **reports no accuracy metrics** ([GitHub](https://github.com/amri-tah/Amazon-Reviews-Sentiment-Analysis)). A Hugging Face Space does transformer-based per-aspect sentiment counts ([HF Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/Saima0/Aspect-Product-Sentiment-Analysis/blob/main/src/streamlit_app.py)). Typical tutorials stop at TextBlob plus a word cloud ([ConsoleFlare](https://consoleflare.com/blog/?p=4076)). The research found **no single open project that combines a lexicon-vs-ML-vs-transformer comparison, NER, dependency-rule aspect sentiment and multi-review summarization with an evaluation section**. That combination is the project's novelty.

The resume space is the most crowded. `srbhr/Resume-Matcher` has about **28.8k stars**, an LLM-backed workflow and a no-LLM ATS score ([GitHub](https://github.com/srbhr/Resume-Matcher)), and Streamlit "ATS score checker" clones are common on Hugging Face ([HF Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/Bodyless/Resume_ATS_Score_Checker/blob/main/src/streamlit_app.py)). Its most novel available angle is an Indian-name bias probe, inspired by a study where embedding retrievers favoured white-associated names 85% of the time ([arXiv 2407.20371](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2407.20371)). Compelling as that is, it adds a sensitive ethics experiment to an already stretched timeline. Plagiarism tools also abound, from JPlag and Dolos for code to Python side-by-side sentence highlighters ([GitHub topic](https://github.com/topics/plagiarism-detector)). Its differentiators are a highlighter that labels each match "copy" or "paraphrase", an N×N class-batch heatmap, and a cross-lingual English–Hindi demo using `paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2` ([SBERT docs](https://www.sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html)).

For the recommended project, the technical stack and the differentiators fit together. spaCy `en_core_web_sm` (NER F 84.56, POS accuracy 97.25, with ORG/PRODUCT/PERSON/GPE labels) handles tagging, parsing and entities in one pipeline ([HF spaCy card](https://huggingface.co/spacy/en_core_web_sm)). Aspect sentiment needs only about 60 lines of rule code. The code pairs adjectives with nouns through `amod` ("great camera"), follows `acomp` to the verb's `nsubj` ("battery is good"), and flips polarity on a `neg` child, which is the method used in a published food-review system ([arXiv 2201.10978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10978)). The team should be honest that such rules miss many aspects ([SlideShare 2018](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/aspect-extraction-performance-with-common-pattern-of-dependency-relation-in-multi-aspect-sentiment-analysis/91662569)), and a small manual precision/recall table turns that weakness into a reported finding. The highest-value extras are a "Compare" tab that runs all models on one input and highlights disagreements, green/red word highlighting from logistic-regression coefficients, a detector for reviews whose star rating contradicts their text, and separate "liked/disliked" extractive summaries. Live Amazon/Flipkart scraping, transformer ABSA and fine-tuning should stay in future work.

## Difficulty, risk and a 16-day plan for two people

The three projects differ less in raw difficulty than in where the risk sits. The ratings below are the research team's judgement, not measured figures:

| | Review Analyzer (1) | Resume–JD Matcher (3) | Plagiarism Checker (12) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Difficulty | Moderate | Moderate, slightly harder | Low to moderate |
| Core build time | About 2 weeks with scope frozen by day 8 | About 2 weeks, little slack | About 8–10 working days |
| Biggest risks | Scope creep; transformer speed on CPU; missing neutral class in IMDB; negations removed as stopwords | Two-column/scanned PDF extraction; skill synonyms; weak ground truth; privacy of real resumes | Clough dataset access; threshold tuning; low MRPC numbers; over-claiming AI-text detection |
| Heavy dependencies | Optional only (DistilBERT, 67M params) | MiniLM (22.7M params); PDF libraries | MiniLM (22.7M params) |

The review analyzer's risks all have known, cheap mitigations. Evaluate transformers only on a 1–2k subset and cache them with `st.cache_resource`. Keep "not", "no" and "never" out of the stopword list. Cap uploaded CSVs at a few thousand rows. Pin spaCy 3.7.x with its matching model. Streamlit Community Cloud's free tier has documented memory limits of roughly 690 MB–2.7 GB ([Streamlit docs](https://docs.streamlit.io/knowledge-base/deploy/resource-limits)), so demo locally and treat deployment as optional. The resume matcher's risks are harder to remove. In one test, all four common Python PDF libraries **scrambled at least one page**, and pdfplumber failed a plain two-column layout at default settings ([Python in Plain English](https://python.plainenglish.io/pymupdf-vs-pdfplumber-vs-pypdf-vs-pypdfium2-all-4-got-every-word-all-4-scrambled-one-page-d12054be37bd)). MiniLM **truncates input beyond 256 word pieces** ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2)), so whole resumes must be chunked. The once-standard parser pyresparser has not been updated since **December 2019** and supports only Python 3.3–3.7 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pyresparser/)).

The recommended split gives each student one coherent half, with both owning the viva:

| Dates | Rachit: models and evaluation | Manan: app and linguistics |
|---|---|---|
| 20–21 Oct | Download IMDB and a 5k–20k Flipkart or Amazon Fine Food sample; cleaning script; EDA plots | Streamlit skeleton (text box, CSV uploader, tabs); word count, top-10 words, word cloud |
| 22–24 Oct | TF-IDF + MNB/LR/LinearSVC, cross-validation, n-gram and preprocessing ablations; save the best model | VADER + TextBlob (3-class thresholds + subjectivity); spaCy NER tab |
| 25–27 Oct | VADER/TextBlob/DistilBERT-subset evaluation; confusion matrices; comparison table; bigram-LM perplexity classifier | Dependency-rule aspect extraction + aspect chart; POS/adjective frequency; noun chunks |
| 28–29 Oct | Summarization (sumy LexRank/LSA/Luhn + own frequency method) with ROUGE; 3-class product experiment | Summary tab (overall + pros/cons); explainability highlighting; CSV download |
| 30 Oct | Both hand-label 50 sentences each for aspect evaluation | Integration, caching, error handling, 5–10k-row test |
| 31 Oct – 3 Nov | Report: results, discussion, related work | Report: system design, screenshots, architecture; README, requirements.txt |
| 4 Nov | Buffer: freeze code, swap modules, rehearse demo and viva | Same |

Features freeze on 27 October. Each student should read and explain the other's code during 29–30 October, because examiners routinely ask a student about the module they did not write.

## Viva questions cluster around "why", "how measured" and "where it fails"

Across all three projects, the predicted questions probe the reasons behind each design choice, how the numbers were obtained, and whether each student understands the internals. For the recommended project, these are the questions to rehearse:

| Likely question | What a strong answer contains |
|---|---|
| What do TF and IDF mean, and why TF-IDF over bag-of-words? | IDF down-weights common words; sublinear TF, `max_df`; the vectorizer is fit on train only to avoid leakage |
| Why does VADER score lower than your trained model on IMDB? | Social-media lexicon tuned with ±0.05 compound thresholds ([vaderSentiment](https://github.com/cjhutto/vaderSentiment)); long multi-aspect reviews, sarcasm, no domain training |
| IMDB has no neutral class, so how do you predict "neutral"? | Lexicon thresholds; 3-class training on 1–2★ / 3★ / 4–5★ product data |
| Did you remove "not" as a stopword? | No: a custom list keeps negations; show the ablation; bigrams and the `neg` dependency handle "not good" |
| Explain `amod`, `acomp` and `nsubj` in your aspect rule, and give a sentence where it fails | "great camera", "battery is good"; failure on implicit aspects like "it dies by noon" |
| Polarity vs subjectivity: can a review be objective and negative? | TextBlob subjectivity runs 0–1 ([TextBlob docs](https://textblob.readthedocs.io/en/dev/quickstart.html)); a factual complaint is objective and negative |
| Why does spaCy tag some brands as PERSON or GPE? | The small model's NER F is about 85% with no domain fine-tuning |
| How does LexRank pick sentences? Why are your ROUGE scores low? | PageRank over a sentence-similarity graph; short abstractive references; compare against a lead-sentence baseline |
| Why smoothing in an N-gram LM? Laplace vs Kneser-Ney? What is perplexity? | Zero counts for unseen n-grams; how probability mass is redistributed; perplexity as inverse geometric-mean probability |
| Why not just use BERT? | 512-token limit, CPU cost, domain shift (80.6% vs 96.0% on IMDB); fine-tuning is impractical on a CPU in this timeline |
| What is new compared with existing GitHub projects? | The same-split model comparison, aspect sentiment, pros/cons summaries, Indian data, and a full evaluation section |

Expect a live demo trap: an examiner may type something like "not bad at all" or a sarcastic review. The right response is to show the disagreement in the Compare tab and explain why lexicons fail on such inputs. A graceful explanation of a failure scores better than a lucky correct answer. If the team switches to the plagiarism checker instead, the equivalent core is the math of Jaccard vs containment vs cosine, the winnowing guarantee that any shared substring of length at least w + k − 1 is detected ([Schleimer et al.](https://sschleimer.warwick.ac.uk/Maths/winnowing.html)), threshold tuning on a train split, and an honest "no" to whether the tool detects ChatGPT text. OpenAI withdrew its own classifier after it caught only **26% of AI-written text** ([Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/openai-shuts-down-flawed-ai-detector/492565)).

## Conclusion

The choice turns less on which project is most impressive than on which turns lab exercises into findings with the least wasted effort. The review analyzer wins because its weaknesses are themselves reportable results. The gap between VADER and logistic regression, the circular Flipkart pseudo-labels, the missing neutral class and the aspect rules' low recall each become a paragraph of honest analysis, not a crisis. The resume matcher has the opposite problem: its central output cannot be validated well, so the most effort would go into the part examiners trust least. The plagiarism checker is the one serious alternative, and it is the right call only if the instructor wants a less familiar topic or values N-gram depth over sentiment and summarization coverage.

The gap the research found is the real opportunity. No published source compares lexicon, classical and transformer sentiment models on the same IMDB split, so a careful two-week student project can produce a table that does not exist elsewhere. That table is what moves a "common" topic above the typical submission. It will be finished only if features freeze on 27 October and the remaining days go to evaluation, the report and viva rehearsal rather than to new features.
